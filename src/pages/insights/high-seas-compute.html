Data centers on the high seas: the legal questions behind wave-powered computing

By Sarah M. Reiter · November 2026 · 3 min read

NOAA Ocean Exploration, 2026 Cook Islands ROV Exploration. Polymetallic nodules, abyssal plain.

In November 2026 Panthalassa opened a pilot factory near the Columbia River in Vancouver, Washington. Its product: data centers that live at sea.

The Portland company's unanchored nodes turn wave energy into electricity, run AI inference on board and send results ashore by satellite. No power goes to shore. In May it announced a $140 million Series B led by Peter Thiel. It plans North Pacific pilots and targets commercial deployment in 2027.

The engineering may be the easier half. The nodes are headed into water where the law is thin. Investors, insurers and regulators will ask five questions. The time to work through them is before deployment.

1. What is a node in law?

The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) assigns rights by category but never defines "ship." A ship flies a flag and enjoys freedom of navigation; an "installation" in a coastal state's exclusive economic zone falls under that state's exclusive jurisdiction (Article 60). U.S. law adds a test: under Lozman v. City of Riviera Beach (2013), a craft not designed to carry people or things over water may not be a vessel at all.

An untethered node fits no category cleanly. Yet the label decides who may board it, which safety rules apply and whether a coastal state can demand permission.

2. Whose law applies, and where?

A coastal state is sovereign in its territorial sea and controls wave energy and installations in its exclusive economic zone. On the high seas the anchor is the flag state, and a craft with no flag has no state's protection. Data adds a layer: the U.S. CLOUD Act reaches data held by providers subject to U.S. jurisdiction, wherever the servers sit. "Beyond any jurisdiction" is a risk, not a feature.

3. What review does deployment trigger?

In U.S. waters, a platform that sends no power ashore has a credible argument, untested for a commercial fleet, that neither a FERC license nor a BOEM lease applies. The argument still needs documenting: an unclear jurisdictional basis is itself a financing and insurance risk.

On the high seas, the BBNJ Agreement (the High Seas Treaty) has been in force since January 2026. Part IV requires parties to screen, and where warranted fully assess, planned activities under their jurisdiction or control. The United States has signed but not ratified, so exposure turns on the fleet's flag and on any party state with jurisdiction or control over it. The first Conference of the Parties meets in January 2027, and early movers will shape how these duties apply to fleets.

4. What science will regulators ask for?

Unmoored nodes avoid mooring-line entanglement. Noise, lighting that draws seabirds and collision risk remain. Fleets add a harder question: cumulative effects. One node is a rounding error; thousands across a migration corridor are not. Baselines and a monitoring plan, offered before anyone asks, buy speed.

5. What happens at end of life?

Deliberately disposing of a platform at sea is "dumping" under the London Convention, and the U.S. Ocean Dumping Act reaches U.S.-flagged vessels everywhere. UNCLOS requires abandoned installations in an exclusive economic zone to be removed for navigational safety, under international standards that allow partial removal. A plan for recovering lost nodes belongs in the design. Diligence teams will ask for it.

The strategic point. Regulatory uncertainty is a risk you can manage like any other: map it, rank it, reduce it. Getting to regulators first is cheaper than hearing from them first.
