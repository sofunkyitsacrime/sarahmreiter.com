Data centers on the high seas: the legal questions behind wave-powered computing

Sarah M. Reiter · November 2026 · 8 min read

In May 2026, Portland-based Panthalassa closed a $140 million Series B, led by Peter Thiel, to build autonomous floating nodes that turn wave energy into electricity and use it to run AI inference at sea, sending results ashore by satellite. After testing prototypes in Washington's Strait of Juan de Fuca, the company has said it plans pilot nodes in the North Pacific, with commercial deployment to follow from 2027. This month it opened a pilot factory in Vancouver, Washington.

Whatever one thinks of the business case, the engineering is the easier half. These are energy plants, computing facilities and floating objects all at once, and they will operate in the part of the planet with the least settled law. Investors, insurers and eventually regulators will ask five questions. The companies that answer them first will help set the terms for everyone else.

1. What is a compute node in law?

The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) assigns rights and duties by category, but it never defines "ship." Is an autonomous, self-propelled or drifting node a ship, entitled to a flag and to freedom of navigation? Is it an "installation or structure," which coastal states control within their exclusive economic zones (Article 60) and which states may build on the high seas only as "permitted under international law" (Article 87)? Or is it something new, like a data buoy or an ocean observing platform?

The answer isn't academic. It decides who may board or inspect a node, what safety and navigation rules apply, and whether a coastal state can require permission. Sea ice raised the same problem a decade ago, when negotiators had to decide how the IMO's Polar Code should treat it. Characterization comes first, and everything else follows from it.

2. Whose law applies, and where?

A node in a state's territorial sea sits under that state's full sovereignty. In its exclusive economic zone, the coastal state controls energy production from waves and currents and the installations that serve it. On the high seas, the main link is the flag state, if the node can carry a flag at all. A company that deploys across zones may face a different regulator, review and liability regime in each one. And because these nodes process data, questions of data jurisdiction and export control follow the hardware out to sea.

3. What review does deployment trigger?

Near shore, the familiar machinery applies. In U.S. waters that can mean federal licensing for marine energy, Coast Guard navigation requirements, protected-species consultation and state coastal-zone review. Beyond national jurisdiction, something new applies: the BBNJ Agreement (the High Seas Treaty), in force since January 2026, which requires its parties to screen and, where warranted, assess planned activities under their jurisdiction or control that may affect the marine environment of the high seas. How states apply those duties to commercial fleets of autonomous platforms is an open question, and early movers will shape the precedent.

4. What science will regulators ask for?

Wave-energy devices raise familiar questions: underwater noise, entanglement risk for whales and turtles, effects on seabirds, and changes to local wave climate. Fleets add a newer one: cumulative effects. One node is a rounding error; thousands across a migration corridor are not. Environmental review has historically been weak at counting cumulative impacts. A company that brings credible baseline data and a monitoring design before being asked will move faster than one that waits.

5. What happens at end of life?

Hardware that is lost, abandoned or scuttled at sea runs into marine-debris and dumping rules, including the London Convention and Protocol, as well as rising expectations about recovery. Decommissioning should be a design input, not a footnote, and it is a question diligence teams increasingly ask.

The strategic point. In frontier ocean industries, regulatory uncertainty is a risk you can manage like any other: map it, rank it and reduce it. The ventures that do so early get better siting, easier permitting and a seat at the table when the rules are written.
