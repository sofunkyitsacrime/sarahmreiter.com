The translation problem

Scientists, lawyers and policymakers describe the same ocean in three different languages.

Projects stall in the gaps between them: a permit that asks for science nobody has collected, a legal brief built on a study it misreads, a policy that can't be monitored. Sarah works in those gaps.

Signature practice

The ocean is becoming infrastructure. The law hasn't caught up.

Wave-powered AI computing, autonomous platforms, ocean carbon removal, seabed minerals. New ocean ventures are moving faster than the frameworks meant to govern them. For these companies, regulatory uncertainty is the main risk, and early, science-literate legal strategy is the main way to reduce it.

Five questions every frontier ocean venture must answer

  1. What is it in law? A ship, an installation or something new?
  2. Whose law applies? Flag state, coastal state or the high seas.
  3. What review does it trigger? At home and beyond national jurisdiction.
  4. What science will regulators demand? And when.
  5. What happens at end of life? Recovery, debris, dumping rules.

Credentials

An oceanographer, an attorney and a published researcher, in one advisor.

Sarah trained as an oceanographer at the U.S. Naval Academy and served as an Air Force operational weather officer. She went on to earn a J.D. in ocean and coastal law, and has spent two decades turning ocean data into decisions. Her work has informed the IMO Polar Code, the High Seas Treaty (BBNJ) negotiations, and International Seabed Authority deliberations on deep-sea mining.
